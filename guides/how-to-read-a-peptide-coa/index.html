How to Read a Peptide COA (Certificate of Analysis)

A certificate of analysis (COA) is the lab's report on one batch of a product. It's the single most useful document a supplier can give you, but only if you know which parts matter. This guide walks through a typical peptide COA from top to bottom.

Published by Care Peptides on 11 Jul 2026 · Last reviewed 5 Nov 2026

Quick answer

Check that the product name, strength and batch (lot) number match what you're buying. Then look at the two results that matter most: HPLC purity (how clean the sample is) and mass spectrometry (whether it's the right molecule). A trustworthy COA names the testing method for each result and ideally shows the chromatogram and spectrum, not just the numbers.

Key takeaways

  • Match the batch first: A COA only describes the batch printed on it. If the lot number doesn't match your vial, it isn't your COA.
  • Purity and identity are different tests: HPLC tells you how pure the sample is; mass spec tells you what it is. You want both.
  • Look for the evidence: Graphs, methods and dates are what separate a real lab report from a typed-up number.

1. The header: what and which batch

The top of a COA identifies exactly what was tested. Look for:

  • Product name and, ideally, the sequence or molecular formula.
  • Batch or lot number: the most important identifier on the page.
  • Test date and sometimes a manufacture or retest date.
  • Who tested it: the manufacturer's own QC lab or an independent lab.

If any of these are missing, treat the rest of the document with caution.

2. The results table

Most COAs list each test in a row with four columns: the test (e.g. purity), the method (e.g. HPLC), the specification or acceptance limit (e.g. ≥ 98.0%), and the result actually measured (e.g. 99.2%). A result should always sit next to the method that produced it. A bare percentage with no method tells you very little.

3. Purity by HPLC

High-performance liquid chromatography separates the peptide from impurities and reports the main peak as a percentage of everything detected. For research peptides, results above 98% are common. A good COA includes the chromatogram itself: one tall main peak with only small peaks around it. See our HPLC purity guide for how to read one.

4. Identity by mass spectrometry

Mass spec confirms the molecule has the expected molecular weight. The COA should state the expected (theoretical) mass and the observed mass, and they should agree closely. HPLC can't do this job: a sample can be 99% pure and still be the wrong peptide. Our mass spectrometry guide explains the numbers.

5. Other tests you might see

  • Appearance: usually “white to off-white lyophilised powder”.
  • Water content (Karl Fischer): freeze-dried peptides still hold some moisture.
  • Peptide content (net peptide): the share of the powder that is actually peptide, as opposed to water and counter-ions such as acetate or TFA salts. This is often 70–90% even when HPLC purity is 99%, and that's normal. The two numbers measure different things.

6. Red flags

  • No batch number, or a batch number that doesn't match your vial.
  • A result of exactly 100.00%, which is very rare in genuine analysis.
  • No methods listed, or no chromatogram or spectrum anywhere.
  • Observed mass that doesn't match the expected mass.
  • The same COA reused, unchanged, for different products or years.
  • Signs of editing, such as mismatched fonts or blurred areas around numbers.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between purity and peptide content?

Purity (by HPLC) is the share of detected material that is the target peptide. Peptide content is the share of the total powder weight that is peptide, after allowing for water and salts. A sample can be 99% pure with 80% peptide content.

Is 99% purity much better than 98%?

Both are high. More important is that the result comes with a method, a chromatogram and a matching batch number.

What if the lot number on my vial doesn't match the COA?

Ask the supplier for the COA for your batch. With Care Peptides, message Kyle with the SKU and lot number and we'll tell you what documentation exists for it.
