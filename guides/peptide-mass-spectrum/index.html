Mass Spectrometry for Peptides: Confirming You Have the Right Molecule

HPLC tells you how clean a sample is. Mass spectrometry tells you what it is. This guide explains how to read the mass-spec section of a peptide certificate, including the charge-state numbers that confuse most people.

Published by Care Peptides on 11 Jul 2026 · Last reviewed 5 Nov 2026

Quick answer

A mass spectrometer measures the mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) of ionised molecules. On a COA you should see the peptide's expected molecular weight next to the observed value, and they should match closely. If the spectrum shows peaks at about half or a third of the molecular weight, that's normal: they're the same molecule carrying two or three charges.

Key takeaways

  • It confirms identity: Matching mass is strong evidence the right peptide was made.
  • Charge states are normal: One peptide can produce several peaks at different m/z values.
  • It's not a purity test: Use HPLC for purity. Mass spec answers a different question.

How it works, briefly

The sample is turned into charged ions, usually by electrospray ionisation (ESI) or MALDI. The instrument then sorts the ions by mass-to-charge ratio and counts them. The result is a spectrum: m/z along the bottom, signal strength up the side.

Understanding charge states

With ESI, peptides often pick up more than one proton, so the same molecule appears at several m/z values. For a peptide with molecular weight M carrying n extra protons, the peak appears at roughly (M + n × 1.007) ÷ n.

Example: BPC-157 has a molecular weight of about 1419.5 Da. You'd expect to see it near m/z 1420.5 as [M+H]+ and near m/z 710.8 as [M+2H]2+. Both peaks point to the same molecule. MALDI spectra tend to show mainly the singly charged [M+H]+ ion.

Expected vs observed mass

A COA should state both. Small differences are normal and depend on the instrument: high-resolution instruments agree to within a few parts per million, while routine ones are often within about ±1 Da for a peptide this size. Also note whether the figure is the monoisotopic mass (lightest isotopes only) or the average mass. They differ by around 1 Da for mid-size peptides, which can look like a mismatch when it isn't.

What mass spec can't tell you

  • How much impurity there is. Peak heights aren't a reliable purity measure.
  • Molecules with the same mass. Some sequence errors or isomers weigh exactly the same.
  • Water and salt content.

That's why a complete COA pairs mass spec with HPLC purity.

Red flags in the mass-spec section

  • An “MS: conforms” line with no numbers and no spectrum.
  • No expected mass stated, so there's nothing to compare against.
  • Observed mass that's far off and not explained by charge state or isotope type.
  • A spectrum with unlabelled axes or no batch number.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my COA show a peak at half the molecular weight?

That's the doubly charged ion [M+2H]2+. The molecule's mass is divided by its charge, so it appears at roughly half the m/z. It's the same peptide.

How close should observed and expected mass be?

Within the accuracy of the instrument. That's often within about 1 Da on routine equipment, and much closer on high-resolution systems. Check whether both figures use the same mass type (monoisotopic or average).

Can mass spec replace HPLC?

No. Mass spec confirms identity; HPLC measures purity. A good COA includes both.
