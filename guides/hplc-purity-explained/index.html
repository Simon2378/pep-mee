HPLC Purity Explained: How to Read a Peptide Chromatogram

Almost every peptide certificate quotes a purity figure “by HPLC”. This guide explains what that test actually does, how to read the chromatogram behind the number, and where its limits are.

Published by Care Peptides on 11 Jul 2026 · Last reviewed 5 Nov 2026

Quick answer

HPLC pushes a dissolved sample through a column that separates molecules by how strongly they stick to it. A detector draws a peak for each thing that comes out. Purity is the area of the main peak divided by the area of all the peaks. A clean result shows one dominant peak; impurities show up as smaller peaks either side of it.

Key takeaways

  • It's a relative measure: HPLC purity compares the main peak with other detected peaks. It doesn't weigh water or salts.
  • The chromatogram is the evidence: A percentage without the graph and method behind it is hard to trust.
  • It doesn't prove identity: A pure sample of the wrong molecule still scores well. Pair HPLC with mass spec.

How the test works

Peptides are usually analysed by reversed-phase HPLC. The sample is dissolved and pumped through a column packed with a water-repelling material (commonly C18). The liquid running through starts mostly watery and gradually becomes more organic (typically acetonitrile with a little trifluoroacetic acid). Different molecules let go of the column at different points, so they come out at different times. A UV detector, usually set around 214–220 nm where peptide bonds absorb light, records each one as a peak.

Reading the chromatogram

  • X-axis: retention time (minutes). When each component came out of the column.
  • Y-axis: detector signal. How strongly it absorbed UV light.
  • Main peak: the target peptide. It should be tall and sharp.
  • Small peaks: impurities. Their combined area is what keeps purity below 100%.
  • Integration table: lists each peak's time and area %. The main peak's area % is the purity figure.

What the impurities usually are

Peptides are built one amino acid at a time, so the common impurities are close relatives of the target: deletion sequences (one amino acid missing), truncated chains, and molecules with leftover protecting groups. Over time, oxidation (often at methionine) and deamidation (at asparagine or glutamine) can also appear. Because they're so similar, they often elute right next to the main peak.

What HPLC can't tell you

  • Identity: it doesn't confirm which molecule the main peak is. That's the job of mass spectrometry.
  • Water and salts: these don't show up, so purity isn't the same as peptide content.
  • Hidden impurities: anything that comes out at the same time as the main peak, or doesn't absorb UV, can be missed.
  • Comparisons between labs: different columns, gradients and wavelengths can give slightly different numbers for the same sample.

What a good HPLC report includes

The chromatogram image, the integration table, and the method conditions: column type, mobile phases, gradient, flow rate and detection wavelength. Plus the batch number and test date so you can tie it to a specific product. For the rest of the certificate, see how to read a peptide COA.

Frequently asked questions

Why is purity never 100%?

Peptide synthesis always leaves traces of closely related by-products. Real results are typically in the high 90s; an exact 100.00% is a warning sign rather than a selling point.

Does high HPLC purity confirm it's the right peptide?

No. HPLC measures how clean the sample is, not what it is. Mass spectrometry confirms identity.

Why might two labs report different purity for the same batch?

Different columns, gradients and detection wavelengths separate impurities differently. Small differences of a fraction of a percent are normal.
